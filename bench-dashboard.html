How much of the hard tier is just guessing position?

111 items

Correct answers are not uniformly placed: 41.4% of hard items have their answer at index 1 (option B). So “always pick B” scores 0.414 while reading nothing at all, against a 0.336 chance baseline. Measured against that floor, most of our models are doing no work on the hard tier.

Two of our models score exactly the positional baseline

Qwen2.5-1.5B and Qwen3.5-2B both land on 0.414 — precisely what a system that ignores the state achieves. The 0.5B looks better at 0.477 only because it picks option B 67% of the time against a 41% base rate, so its bias pays a dividend the benchmark did not intend. Only Qwen3.5-4B (+0.190) and Gemma 4 E4B (+0.181) genuinely read the document — and the 4B is the least position-biased model we ran, picking index 1 just 32% of the time.

Knowing when to answer

risk–coverage

Accuracy rises monotonically with confidence on every model, and never drops below chance — so inverting low-confidence answers strictly loses. Deferring them does not. AURC is the area under the risk–coverage curve; lower is better.

What we learned the hard way

    Read before quoting any number. This is a public-subset score, not a board submission: 231 of 534 decisions are public, and the judge tier (146 items, 28% of the intelligence weight) is held out and renormalised away. Calibration scores on ECE alone, since the gold distributions ship only with the held-out set. Cost uses our own energy estimate (30 W at $0.30/kWh); the board estimates local weights by size class and is more conservative. Latency is measured on a consumer Mac mini running a desktop OS — the p95 tail is structural (95.8% of a long prompt is state text, and a 3.9K-token prefill genuinely costs ~9 s at 120 GB/s), not background noise. Granting a perfect p95 would move our best score 73.5 → 76.2, against a 17.5-point capability gap.
